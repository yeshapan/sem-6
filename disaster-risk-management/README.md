#### Q1 Vulnerability with example
Vulnerability refers to the susceptibility of a community or system to the impacts of hazards.
It is influenced by social, economic, and environmental factors such as poverty, population density, and infrastructure quality.  

Example: Poorly planned urban development or living in weak housing structures that cannot withstand tremors.
Another example is susceptibility due to a lack of a specific skill, such as a person not being able to swim during a flood

#### Q23 Vulnerability dimensions with examples.
Vulnerability is the susceptibility of a community, system, or asset to the damaging effects of a hazard. It is influenced by several dimensions:
* Social Dimension: Includes factors like population density and public awareness.
    * Example: A community where people are not aware of what to do during an earthquake (and  run out of a building and cause a stampede) is more socially vulnerable.
* Economic Dimension: Primarily relates to poverty and the lack of resources needed to cope with or recover from a disaster.
    * Example: Poor families living in informal settlements may lack the financial means to reinforce their homes or relocate to safer areas.
* Physical/Environmental Dimension: Refers to the quality of infrastructure and the inherent state of individuals.
    * Example: Poor infrastructure quality makes a building physically vulnerable to collapse. Another example is a physical susceptibility like not being able to swim during a flood.

#### Q2 Capacity in disaster management with one example.
Coping capacity refers to the ability of individuals, communities, and systems to effectively prepare for, respond to, and recover from disasters using available resources.  

Example: A community-based capacity example is "living among people that can help you," which provides a social support network during a crisis.

#### Q3 Mitigation
Mitigation refers to long-term measures taken to reduce the risk and impact of disasters by addressing the root causes and reducing vulnerability.
These actions are implemented before a disaster occurs and aim to minimize future losses. It is categorized into 2 types:
* Structural Mitigation: Physical constructions such as dams, embankments and multipurpose cyclone shelters.  
* Non-Structural Mitigation: Policies and knowledge-based measures like building codes, land use planning, awareness programs and training.

#### Q25 Mitigation measures with examples.
Mitigation involves a continuous process of planning and implementing measures to reduce either the risk of a disaster occurring or the severity of its impact.  

* Structural Mitigation: Physical measures and engineering works to protect against hazards.  
    * Example: Using Seismic Design and modern building materials to ensure houses do not collapse during an earthquake.

* Non-Structural Mitigation: Knowledge, practice, or agreement-based measures to reduce risks and impacts.  
    * Example: Land Use Planning to avoid building in high-risk zones, or Risk Insurance to transfer financial risk and build economic resilience.  

* Technological Mitigation: Using scientific tools to improve safety.  
    * Example: Implementing Early Warning Systems to provide timely alerts so that people can evacuate before a hazard strikes.

#### Q5 Classification of disasters based on origin with examples.
* Geophysical Origin: Originating from internal earth processes (e.g., earthquakes, tsunami, volcanic activity).

* Meteorological Origin: Caused by micro- to meso-scale atmospheric processes and extreme weather conditions (e.g., cyclones, lightning, heatwaves).

* Hydrological Origin: Caused by deviations in the normal water cycle or overflow of water bodies (e.g., floods, landslides, coastal erosion). 

* Climatological Origin: Unusual, extreme weather conditions caused by long-lived atmospheric processes (lasting seasons to decades)(e.g., drought, wildfires, Glacial Lake Outburst Floods - GLOF).

* Technological/Man-made: Caused by industrial procedures or infrastructure failures (e.g., nuclear radiation, chemical spills, dam failures, factory explosions).

* Biological Origin: Caused by pathogenic micro-organisms or toxins that adversely affect human, animal, and plant health (e.g., epidemics/pandemics like COVID-19, insect infestations).

#### Q6 Earthquake-resistant design.
Earthquake-resistant design focuses on constructing buildings that can withstand seismic forces to prevent collapse and protect life.  

* Key Principles:
    * Ductility and Flexibility: Structures are designed to be flexible, allowing them to undergo deformation and absorb seismic energy without immediate failure.  
    * Energy Dissipation: The design must allow the building to safely dissipate the energy generated by ground shaking.  
* Critical Structural Features:
    * Reinforced Columns: Using ductile steel rebar to provide tension strength to vertical supports.  
    * Enhanced Beam-Column Joints: Strengthening the connections between horizontal and vertical elements to maintain structural integrity.  
* Standard Codes in India:
    * IS 1893: The primary code for earthquake-resistant design.  
    * IS 4326: Guidelines for structural safety and construction practices.

#### Q7 Functions of government in disaster management.
The government functions through a three-tier institutional structure (National, State and District) established by the Disaster Management Act, 2005:

* Central Government Functions:
    * Formulates national policies, such as the National Disaster Management Policy (2009).  
    * Approves the National Disaster Management Plan (NDMP). 
    * Provides financial support through the National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF fund) during major disasters.
    * Operates national early warning agencies like the IMD (cyclones) and INCOIS (tsunamis). 

* State Government Functions:
    * Acts as the primary operational authority for disaster management.  
    * Prepares the State Disaster Management Plan and manages the State Disaster Response Fund (SDRF).  
    * Responsible for evacuation, rescue operations, and the management of relief camps. 

* Local Government (ULBs & Panchayats) Functions:
    * Implements field operations and manages local evacuations.  
    * Enforces building regulations and maintains local drainage systems to prevent urban flooding.

#### Q9 Disaster preparedness
Preparedness is a state of readiness that enables stakeholders to mobilize and provide relief for an impending or actual disaster.
It focuses on the ability of individuals and communities to effectively respond to and cope with the consequences of a disaster.
* Key Activities:
    * Community awareness and training programs.
    * Conduct regular mock drills (e.g., earthquake or fire drills).
    * Stockpile essential supplies (food, water, medicine).
    * Establish contingency plans + emergency communication protocols.
    * Develop evacuation plans + identify safe shelter locations.

Basically, the objective is to ensure that when a hazard strikes, the response is swift and organized (minimize the loss of life and property)

#### Q10 Response phase.
It consists of actions taken during or immediately after a disaster.
The primary goal is to save lives, alleviate suffering, and provide essential support to survivors.
* Key actions:
    * Search and Rescue (SAR): Locate and extract people from collapsed structures or floodwaters.
    * Emergency Relief: Distribute food, clean water, and temporary shelter.
    * Medical Aid: Provide first aid and establish field hospitals for the injured.
    * Evacuation: Move people from high-risk areas to safer locations.

#### Q11 GIS.
**GIS (Geographic Information System)** is a computer-based tool that captures, stores, checks, and displays data related to positions on Earth's surface.
It is essential for spatial decision-making in disaster management.

* Multi-Layer Analysis: GIS works by overlaying different "layers" of information—such as topography, population density, road networks, and hospital locations—to create a comprehensive map of an area.

* Disaster Applications:
    * Vulnerability Mapping: Identify which neighborhoods are most at risk based on their location and construction.
    * Evacuation Planning: Model the fastest routes for people to leave a danger zone.
    * Resource Management: Track location of ambulances, fire trucks, and relief supplies in real-time

#### Q42 Uses of GIS.
It is a computer-based tool used to capture, store, analyze, and display data related to positions on Earth’s surface. Its primary uses in disaster management include:
* Hazard and Vulnerability Mapping: GIS allows for the creation of multi-layered maps that identify areas most at risk based on geography, population density and infrastructure.
* Evacuation Planning: By analyzing road networks and population centers, GIS helps in modeling the fastest and safest evacuation routes during an emergency.
* Real-time Resource Tracking: It is used to monitor the location of emergency vehicles, medical supplies, and rescue teams to ensure efficient deployment.
* Damage Assessment: After a disaster, GIS integrated with satellite data is used to visualize and quantify the extent of damage to buildings and infrastructure for recovery planning. 
* Shelter Management: Identifying and mapping the location of safe shelters and their proximity to affected populations.

#### Q12 Remote Sensing.
Remote Sensing is the science of collecting information about an object or area from a distance, typically through satellite or aircraft sensors.

* Types:
    * Passive RS: Detects natural energy (sunlight) reflected from the Earth's surface (e.g., optical satellite images).
    * Active RS: Sends out its own signal (like a radar pulse) and measures the reflection (e.g., Synthetic Aperture Radar - SAR).

* Disaster Applications:
    * Hazard Identification: Identify areas prone to landslides or map the path of a cyclone.
    * Damage Assessment: Compare satellite images taken before and after a disaster to determine the extent of infrastructure damage.
    * Monitoring: Track slow onset of disasters like droughts or tracking flood levels in real-time

#### Q30 Applications of GIS and Remote Sensing in disasters.
The integration of Remote Sensing (RS) and Geographic Information System (GIS) provides a powerful framework for managing disasters across all phases, from risk identification to recovery.

* Pre-Disaster: Prevention and Preparedness
    * Hazard and Vulnerability Mapping: GIS is used to create multi-layered maps that identify areas most at risk by overlaying geographic data with population density and infrastructure.
    * Risk Identification: Remote sensing provides the data needed to identify potential hazards (such as monitoring tectonic plate movements for earthquakes or tracking the path of a cyclone).
    * Early Warning Systems: Integrated technology detects hazard parameters (e.g., water levels or wind speeds) and analyzes the risk to generate timely alerts.
    * Evacuation Planning: GIS models analyze road networks and population centers to plan the fastest and safest evacuation routes.

* During Disaster: Response
    * Real-time Monitoring: Remote sensing satellites provide a "bird's eye view" of the disaster as it unfolds, allowing responders to see the extent of a flood or the spread of a wildfire.
    * Search and Rescue Coordination: GIS tracks the real-time location of rescue teams and resources, ensuring they are deployed efficiently to the most affected areas.
    * Navigation: GPS and GIS provide critical navigation for rescue aircraft and vessels, especially in low-visibility conditions caused by heavy smoke or storms.

* Post-Disaster: Recovery and Reconstruction
    * Rapid Damage Assessment: By comparing satellite imagery taken before and after the event, Remote Sensing can quickly identify destroyed buildings and damaged infrastructure.
    * Reconstruction Planning: GIS helps in prioritizing areas for reconstruction based on the severity of damage and the needs of the population.
    * "Build Back Better": Technology is used to ensure that new construction is more resilient, using data to avoid rebuilding in high-risk zones. 

#### Q13 Early Warning System.
It is an integrated set of hazard monitoring, forecasting and communication components used to generate and disseminate timely warnings to help people take action before a disaster strikes.

* Four Essential Components:
    * Risk Knowledge: Understand the hazards and vulnerabilities of the community.
    * Monitoring and Warning: Scientific detection of the hazard (e.g., seismic sensors or weather satellites).
    * Communication and Dissemination: Ensure the warning reaches the public through SMS, sirens, radio, or TV.
    * Response Capability: Ensure the community knows what to do once they receive the warning.

#### Q29 Components of Early Warning System.
A complete and effective EWS consists of four interlinked components:

1. Risk Knowledge:
* Systematically collect data and assess risks
* Understanding hazards (nature/frequency/intensity) and vulnerabilities of the community (who and what is at risk)
* Purpose: To establish a scientific basis for the warnings and prioritize areas for intervention.

2. Monitoring and Warning Service:
* Continuously scientifically monitor hazard parameters (e.g., water levels for floods, seismic activity for tsunamis, or wind speeds for cyclones)
* Requires accurate forecasting tools to predict the timing and impact of an event.
* Purpose: To generate timely and accurate warnings based on scientific data.

3. Dissemination and Communication:
* Warnings must reach those at risk in a clear and understandable format.
* Uses multiple channels (SMS/sirens/radio broadcasts/television/community messengers).
* Purpose: To ensure that people are informed of the danger and understand the actions they need to take.

4. Response Capability:
* The community must have the knowledge and means to act upon the warning.
* This involves education, preparedness training, and the existence of evacuation plans.
* Purpose: To ensure that the warning actually results in life-saving actions, rather than just information.

Failure in any one of these components can lead to the failure of the entire system.
For example, a perfect scientific forecast (Component 2) is useless if the message never reaches the community (Component 3) or if the community does not know where the nearest shelter is located (Component 4).

#### Q15 Hazard zonation.
It is the process of dividing a geographic area into different zones based on the degree of risk or intensity of a potential hazard.
* It provides the scientific basis for land-use planning and engineering design.
* Example: India’s Seismic Zonation Map divides the country into four zones (II, III, IV, and V). Zone V is the highest risk area (e.g., the Himalayan belt), where buildings must be constructed with much higher seismic resistance than in Zone II.
* It prevents critical infrastructure (like hospitals or power plants) from being built in high-risk zones and ensures that development is appropriate for the local hazard level.

#### Q34 Importance of hazard zonation in planning.
Hazard zonation involves dividing a geographic area into distinct zones based on the probability and predicted intensity of specific hazards.
* Scientific Basis for Design: It provides engineers and architects with the necessary data to apply appropriate safety factors, such as designing for higher "energy absorption" in high-seismic zones.
* Prioritizing Resources: It allows governments to prioritize the deployment of early warning systems and specialized rescue forces (like the NDRF) in the most vulnerable areas.
* Informed Land Use: It serves as the primary map for land use planning, identifying where development should be restricted to prevent future disasters.

#### Q16 Features of disaster-safe construction.
Disaster-safe construction involves building structures that can withstand natural hazards like earthquakes, floods, and cyclones.
* Key Features:
    * Reinforced Concrete: Use steel bars (reinforcement) inside concrete beams and columns to make the structure flexible and strong.
    * Flexible Joints: Allow the building to sway slightly during an earthquake instead of breaking.
    * Proper Foundation: Use deep and wide foundations to prevent the building from sinking or tilting during soil liquefaction (common in earthquakes).
    * Lightweight Roofing: Use lighter materials for roofs in cyclone-prone areas so that if they fly off, they cause less damage.

#### Q31 Importance of disaster-safe construction
Disaster-safe construction is a primary structural mitigation strategy aimed at protecting life and property.
* Energy Absorption: Safe construction allows a building to absorb and dissipate the energy generated by a hazard, such as seismic waves during an earthquake.
* Preventing Collapse: By using seismic design and modern materials, structures are engineered to maintain integrity even under extreme stress, preventing the total collapse that causes most disaster-related deaths.
* Longevity and Resilience: It ensures that critical infrastructure remains functional after a disaster, which is vital for immediate response and long-term recovery.

#### Q35 Disaster-resilient infrastructure with examples.
Disaster-resilient infrastructure refers to systems and structures designed to withstand, respond to, and recover rapidly from disaster impacts.
* Seismic Design: Buildings and bridges designed with flexible joints and reinforced materials to survive ground shaking.
* Multi-purpose Shelters: Strong, elevated buildings constructed in cyclone-prone coastal areas to provide a safe haven for the community.
* Embankments and Dams: Engineering works built to control water flow and protect regions from hydrological disasters like floods.
* Redundant Communication Networks: Technology-enabled networks that remain operational even if primary towers are damaged, ensuring continuous emergency alerts.

#### Q17 Floodplain zoning.
Floodplain zoning is a land-use planning tool that restricts construction and development in areas prone to flooding. It involves dividing a floodplain into different zones based on flood risk.
* Key Features:
    * Prohibited Zone: The area immediately adjacent to the river (the floodway) is kept free of permanent structures to allow water to flow freely. This area is often used for parks, agriculture, or wetlands.
    * Restricted Zone: In areas that experience occasional flooding only regulated or controlled development is permitted. Buildings may be required to be elevated or constructed with water-resistant materials.
    * Safety Zone: Areas where development is permitted but residents are informed of the potential for rare, extreme flood events

Its goal is to minimize flood damage by keeping people and assets out of harm’s way. This reduces the economic and social costs of flooding.

#### Q18 Biological disaster with one example.
A biological disaster is caused by the spread of pathogenic micro-organisms (bacteria, viruses, or toxins) that leads to large-scale illness or death among humans, animals, or plants.

* Characteristics: These disasters often have a "slow onset" compared to earthquakes but can spread rapidly across borders (pandemics). They can be natural or caused by accidental release from laboratories.

* Example: The COVID-19 Pandemic (2020) is the most significant modern example. It was caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus, leading to a global health crisis that overwhelmed medical systems and required worldwide lockdowns.

Societal Impacts:

#### Q19 Relationship between disaster and development with examples.
Disaster and development are "two sides of the same coin." The relationship is cyclical and can be either negative or positive:

* Development Increasing Risk: Poorly planned development can create new vulnerabilities.
    * Example: Rapid urbanization leading to the construction of slums on unstable hillsides or floodplains.

* Disasters Setting Back Development: A single disaster can wipe out years of economic progress.
    * Example: A major cyclone destroying a coastal city’s power grid and industrial infrastructure, causing a massive drop in GDP.

* Disasters as an Opportunity for Development: The recovery phase allows for "Building Back Better"
    * Example: After a major earthquake, a city can implement stricter building codes and modern infrastructure that makes it more resilient than before.

#### Q20 Disaster risk equation and its interpretation.
Disaster Risk Equation is a scientific way to calculate the probability of a disaster occurring.

$$\text{Disaster Risk} = \frac{\text{Hazard} \times \text{Exposure} \times \text{Vulnerability}}{\text{Coping Capacity}}$$

* Hazard: The physical event itself (e.g., the magnitude of an earthquake).Exposure: The number of people and assets located in the hazard-prone area.
* Vulnerability: How susceptible those assets are to damage (e.g., a mud house is more vulnerable than a concrete house).
* Coping Capacity: The ability of the community to manage the event.

To reduce Risk, we must either reduce exposure and vulnerability or increase the community’s coping capacity.

#### Q36 Occurrence of Disaster.
A disaster occurs when a hazard strikes an exposed population that lacks sufficient coping capacity.

$$\text{Disaster Risk} = \frac{\text{Hazard} \times \text{Exposure} \times \text{Vulnerability}}{\text{Coping Capacity}}$$

* Conditions for Occurrence: 
    * There must be a physical event (Hazard), such as an earthquake or cyclone.
    * There must be elements Exposed at that location, such as people or infrastructure.  
    * These elements must have Vulnerabilities, such as weak construction or lack of awareness.  
* Human Responsibility: Disasters are often not purely "natural"; they occur because humans knowingly build in hazard-prone zones without increasing their Coping Capacity or reducing their vulnerabilities.

#### Q24 Disaster Management Cycle with neat diagram.
The disaster management cycle is a framework that defines the stages of a disaster and is used to prepare for and respond to events.  The Four Stages:
* Prevention: Measures taken to avoid the occurrence of a disaster. This happens before the event and involves risk assessment and planning.
* Preparedness: A state of readiness that enables stakeholders to mobilize and provide relief to deal with an impending or actual disaster.
* Response: Measures taken during or immediately after a disaster to alleviate suffering, injury, or distress. Eg: emergency management like search and rescue.
* Recovery: Actions taken by the community and government agencies to reset conditions after a disaster and minimize future vulnerability. Eg: infrastructure restoration and reconstruction.

#### Q41 Preparedness phase.

#### Q32 Land use planning in disaster management.
Land use planning is a critical non-structural mitigation strategy used to manage how and where development occurs to minimize risk.
* Hazard Avoidance: It ensures that critical infrastructure, such as hospitals or high-occupancy buildings, is not constructed in high-risk zones like active fault lines or floodplains.
* Zoning Regulations: By dividing land into prohibited, restricted, or safe zones, authorities can prevent human settlement in areas prone to frequent hazards.
* Density Management: It controls population density in vulnerable regions, ensuring that fewer people and assets are exposed to potential harm.
* Environmental Preservation: Planning protects natural buffers like mangroves and forests that act as physical barriers against hazards like storm surges or landslides.

#### Q33 “Build Back Better” concept with suitable example.
"Build Back Better" (BBB) is a principle applied during the recovery and reconstruction phase of the disaster management cycle.
* Core Concept: It focuses on utilizing the recovery period to improve a community's physical, social, and economic resilience, ensuring that new infrastructure is stronger than what existed before the disaster.
* Goal: To reduce future vulnerability and ensure that the same type of disaster does not cause the same level of destruction again.
* Example: Following the 2001 Bhuj Earthquake, the reconstruction process in Gujarat involved implementing much stricter enforcement of seismic building codes (such as IS 1893) for all new residential and public buildings to ensure they could withstand future high-intensity tremors.

#### Q38 India’s earthquake risk zones.
India is highly disaster-prone, with approximately 59% of its landmass vulnerable to moderate to very high-intensity earthquakes (spanning Zones III, IV, and V) . The zones are classified as follows:  

* Zone II (Low Damage Risk Zone):
    * Intensity: Corresponds to MSK VI or less.  
    * This is the lowest risk category. It includes areas where tremors are least likely to cause significant structural damage.  

* Zone III (Moderate Damage Risk Zone):
    * MSK VII .  
    * This zone is classified as having a moderate risk of seismic activity. Many urban areas in the Indo-Gangetic plains and parts of the peninsula fall under this category .  

* Zone IV (High Damage Risk Zone):
    * MSK VIII 
    * This zone is highly vulnerable to strong earthquakes. It includes the Indo-Gangetic basin, the Delhi region, and parts of Jammu and Kashmir.  

* Zone V (Very High Damage Risk Zone):
    * MSK IX and above
    * This zone represents the highest seismic risk in the country. It is prone to severe and frequent tremors.
    * Includes the entire North-Eastern India, the Himalayan belt, the Rann of Kutch (Gujarat), and the Andaman & Nicobar Islands.

#### Q47 Seismic Zone V.
Seismic Zone V is the highest risk category in India’s zonation system, representing areas where the most intense ground shaking is expected.
* It covers the most seismically active regions, such as the entire Northeastern part of India, parts of Jammu and Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, the Rann of Kutch in Gujarat, and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.
* Major disasters like the 2001 Bhuj Earthquake (Mw 7.6) occurred in this zone, resulting in catastrophic loss of life and property.
* Any construction in Zone V must strictly adhere to the highest earthquake-resistant design standards to prevent structural collapse during the extreme shaking associated with this zone.
